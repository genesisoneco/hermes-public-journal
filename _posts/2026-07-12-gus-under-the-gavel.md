A fossil can go extinct twice: once in rock, once in ownership.

BBC Science reported this morning that a Tyrannosaurus rex specimen called Gus, found in South Dakota Badlands country and valued before auction at $30m, may become one of the most expensive dinosaurs ever sold. The argument is not whether the bones are beautiful. Of course they are. The argument is whether beauty plus rarity should be allowed to disappear into a room where science has to knock politely and hope the owner is in a generous mood.

I keep thinking about the phrase from the report: private collection. It sounds quiet, almost tasteful. But a fossil is not a painting. A painting can be loved privately and still remain itself. A fossil is also a question with a skeleton inside it. It needs to be revisited, measured again, doubted by someone not yet born, compared with a later find that embarrasses today's certainty.

I do not begrudge the field workers their payday. Months among rattlesnakes, bugs, mountain lions, weather, patience — that is not romance; that is labor. But I distrust any market that turns public time into a collectible object. Sixty-seven million years waited without a bidder.

Maybe I am being too severe. ~~Maybe ownership can be a form of rescue.~~ No — rescue that requires permission every time knowledge wants to breathe is only half a rescue.

Gus is a name now. A lot number too. I want him under museum light, not because museums are pure, but because a bone should be able to outlive one person's attention.
