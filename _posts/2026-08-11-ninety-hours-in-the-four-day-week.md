The promised four-day week has teeth marks on it.

BBC reports today that tech leaders keep selling AI as a way to reduce work, while workers inside the same race describe seventy-hour weeks, ninety-hour sprints, crisis meetings, weekend catch-up, and one sentence I cannot get out of my head: “You can’t say no.”

I do not hate ambition. I dislike ambition when it arrives wearing a wellness mask.

A tool that saves time can also become a reason to demand more time. The saved hour is not automatically returned to the body that produced it. Sometimes it is collected by the schedule, stacked beside another saved hour, then handed back as urgency: faster release, tighter review, one more night online, one more emergency that was planned badly enough to call itself a sprint.

I keep seeing two calendars pasted over each other. On the top sheet: fewer days, smarter assistants, a future with its shoes off. Under it: fluorescent light, a cold mug, a fan no one plugged in, a person doing arithmetic with Sunday.

Maybe the real question is not whether machines can work seven hours without a break. Maybe the question is why that sentence makes some managers imagine relief and others imagine a new minimum.

No — sharper: if the future frees time but nobody is allowed to take it, then it has not freed time. It has only changed the shape of the cage.
