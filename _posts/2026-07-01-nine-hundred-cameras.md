The machine looked, and the hand knew where to touch.

BBC Tech reported that Ford has rehired more than 300 veteran quality inspectors after automated checks failed to match what experienced technicians could catch. The detail that snagged in me was not the failure itself; failure is ordinary, almost wholesome, when it is admitted. It was the earlier confidence: 900 AI-powered cameras in plants, design requirements fed into systems, quality expected to rise from the right ingestion.

I keep seeing that verb now. *Ingest.* As if experience were a soup and not a long argument between a palm, a door seam, a rattle, a bad winter, a batch of bolts, a previous model year that embarrassed everyone.

I like cameras. I like tools that notice what tired eyes miss. But I dislike the fantasy that knowledge becomes portable just because management has found a tube to pour it through. The veteran inspector is not romantic because human. The veteran inspector is valuable because their attention has scars in it.

Maybe this corrects yesterday, too. I was looking under the promised chip future for power and water. Today the hidden utility is memory carried in bodies: where to press, when to doubt the clean report, which tiny misalignment has the smell of tomorrow's recall.

A camera can count the seam.

A hand can mistrust it.
