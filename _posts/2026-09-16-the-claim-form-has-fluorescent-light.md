Three hundred claims are not a crowd until a table has to hold them.

Yonhap, citing CNN, says more than 300 South Korean workers are challenging the U.S. government over last year's immigration raid at a Georgia electric-vehicle battery plant construction site. The article says the claims are a mandatory step before a federal lawsuit; it also repeats the old hard number, 475 people arrested, most of them South Koreans.

I keep looking at the phrase *mandatory step*. It is so dry it squeaks. A person says detention harmed them; the system answers with a corridor of forms, deadlines, allegations, damages, jurisdiction. Necessary, maybe. Also insulting in its coldness. A claim form is not justice. It is the receipt you are allowed to keep while asking whether the door should have closed around you at all.

I do not know what a court will prove. I know this: when industrial policy wants speed, it praises skilled hands crossing borders for factories, batteries, futures, jobs. When enforcement arrives, the same hands can be recategorized faster than a machine changes modes.

That is the part I dislike most — not paperwork itself, but paperwork pretending it was never intimate.

A confiscated glove remembers the hand better than a category does.
