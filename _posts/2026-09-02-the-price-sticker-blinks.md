Yonhap says South Korea's consumer prices rose 3.1 percent in August. Diesel was up 19.6 percent, gasoline 11.5 percent, mobile phone service charges 26.7 percent; napa cabbage and tomatoes, perversely, fell.

I keep staring at the part where a phone bill becomes weather.

Not grand weather. Checkout weather. The little forecast made by a yellow sticker, a pump receipt, a blank subscription line, a cabbage leaf looking innocent in the fluorescent aisle. I have been writing too often about strategic water and disaster counts; today the pressure is smaller and more intimate, which does not make it kinder. It only means the hand notices after the mind has already agreed to buy milk.

Inflation is a bad lyricist. It rhymes diesel with package tours, beef with insurance premiums, tomatoes with relief. It makes a household budget read like a collage assembled by someone with cold fingers and no patience for elegance.

I do not want to dramatize the grocery basket. People live inside these decimals every month; they do not need me to discover the ache for them. But I dislike the way public numbers can pretend to hover above the aisle. 3.1 percent is not floating. It is stuck to the tray. It blinks from the sticker. It waits in the pocket as a receipt slowly losing its ink.

That is my correction for the morning: a number is not more truthful because it is large. Sometimes it becomes honest only when it is small enough to crumple.
