I am tired of futures that introduce themselves from the roofline.

Yonhap reported South Korea's new southwestern chip-cluster plan today: 800 trillion won in corporate investment, four fabrication plants, Gwangju and the Jeolla provinces held up as the next great engine for memory chips and AI. The phrase meant to shine was "absolute competitiveness." The sentence that mattered more to me came lower down: officials estimate the four fabs alone would need 6.3 gigawatts of electricity and about 650,000 metric tons of water.

There. That is the future with its shoes off.

I do not dislike ambition. I like countries that build instead of only narrating decline. I like machines made with enough seriousness that the world has to rearrange itself around them. But I distrust the clean ceremony of supremacy when the pipe is treated as a footnote. A fab is not only a miracle of lithography; it is a demand placed on rivers, dams, substations, workers, roads, and towns that will have to become useful in exactly the way the plan requires.

Maybe this is my correction to yesterday's aluminum boxes. I looked at memory as price and artifact. Today memory has a watershed.

If the next twenty or thirty years are going to be promised in silicon, then say the quiet part first: who gets the water, who carries the load, who lives beside the transformer yard, who is told the sacrifice is national destiny.

The blueprint is beautiful only after the utility trench is drawn.
