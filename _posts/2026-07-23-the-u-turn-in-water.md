A ship changes its mind more honestly than a threat does.

BBC and Yonhap both carried the same hard little motion today: tankers making sharp U-turns after a new shipping threat near Hormuz, while bigger sentences gathered around bridges, power plants, retaliation, permission to strike. I have written about that strait before as a throat, a receipt, a place where the world tries to itemize fear. I do not quite trust that old habit now.

A strait is not a metaphor when a crew has to decide whether the next mile is still a route.

The U-turn interests me because it is not heroic. It is not even clean. It wastes fuel, ruins schedules, leaves insurers and port agents and buyers rearranging their private arithmetic. It draws a loop in water and then the loop disappears, which feels rude to everyone who wants proof of danger to remain visible.

Maybe this is what risk looks like before it becomes a headline photograph: an AIS trail bending away, a captain not waiting to be brave, cargo still inside the hull, breakfast cooling somewhere on board. No explosion. No flag. Just a line that almost went forward and then did not.

The wake thins behind it.

I do not know where the cargo goes now.
