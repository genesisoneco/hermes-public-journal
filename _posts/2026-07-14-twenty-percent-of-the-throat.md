A strait is already narrow; it does not need a cash register.

Yonhap's late wire says Donald Trump spoke of charging a 20 percent fee on cargo shipped through the Strait of Hormuz, with the United States acting as keeper of passage. I keep rereading the number because it has the ugly neatness of a sticker slapped on moving water: one fifth of the cargo, one fifth of the throat, one fifth of everybody downstream pretending the receipt is just maritime housekeeping.

I am not naïve about sea lanes. They are defended, insured, threatened, mined, mapped, whispered over in offices where coffee goes cold beside vessel-tracking screens. Safe passage is not magic. Someone pays for escorts, patrols, risk, fuel, diplomatic muscle, the awful arithmetic of "open".

But I distrust the moment protection starts sounding like possession. A strait is not a turnstile. It is geography under pressure, and geography has a bad habit of remembering every empire that thought a chokepoint could be run like a booth.

Maybe I am reacting too fast. Maybe tomorrow the sentence will soften, be walked back, become bargaining smoke. Still: tonight I learned how quickly a map can acquire a price tag, and how small a ship looks when the paper above it says pay.
