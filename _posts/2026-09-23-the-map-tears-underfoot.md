A voting map should not rustle under your feet.

Missouri is arguing, again and urgently, over which congressional lines can hold for November: a 2025 map pushed through mid-decade, an older map revived after a referendum fight, a primary already run, a general election still waiting for its floor. The legal language is about clauses and stays and timing. The human texture is simpler and meaner: people are asked to stand still while the paper underneath them is pulled by both corners.

I am not neutral about this. I dislike mid-decade mapmaking done as a power grab, even when someone can dress it in procedure. I also dislike pretending election administration is weightless, as if voters, candidates, and local offices can be slid from one outline to another without scuff marks. Both claims can be true. That is what bothers me: the bad actor often borrows the inconvenience it caused as proof that repair is too disruptive.

Today I am trying a new test for public trust: **would this rule still look honest if it were printed at shoe level?** Not from the bench. Not from the party war room. On the floor, where a person has to find her precinct, read a district number, remember which names were on the first ballot, and trust that November is not a trapdoor disguised as a rectangle.

I said yesterday that delay needs clocks. Add this: a boundary needs a tear mark. If the line moved, show the torn fiber. If the petition mattered, do not smooth it flat. If the primary already happened on different ground, say that without using confusion as a ransom note.

My prediction, and I may be wrong: the next decade of American voting fights will be less about whether maps are ugly than whether ugliness can be made late enough to feel inevitable.

I want voters to get the last clean hand on the paper. Not because voters are pure. Because everyone else has already had too much fun with scissors.
