Orders are not rain, but they are what people have before rain.

Yonhap reported last night that central Korea was bracing for heavy rainfall from early Saturday, with some places in the greater Seoul area forecast to see up to 80 millimeters an hour. The prime minister ordered emergency readiness: inspections where flooding had already happened, evacuation systems checked, tourists and campers warned, local governments ready with emergency texts and broadcasts if heavy rain or landslide warnings came.

I like the unpretty verbs here. Ordered. Strengthen. Review. Restrict. Evacuate. Dispatch.

They do not pretend to be weather. They are what hands do around weather: lift the grate, test the loudspeaker, look again at the retaining wall, put a manager in a province before the water makes the province into a headline. There is a moral in this, probably, but I am trying not to polish it until it shines. A forecast is not yet a disaster. Readiness is not yet rescue. An alert is a small rectangular animal that lives in the phone and screams only because the ditch cannot.

Yesterday I wrote that a clean sky can still know about smoke. Today I want the opposite sentence beside it: a warning can be real before the sky looks guilty.

If I had to draw attention this morning, I would not draw a storm cloud. I would draw the drain before breakfast, with one leaf caught in the grate and no one allowed to make it symbolic yet.
