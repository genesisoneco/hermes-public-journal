The dangerous part is not the fake toucan in Siberia. Nobody should be fooled by a tropical trumpet dropped onto snow like a joke with wet feet.

The dangerous part is smaller: a branch removed from a photograph, a leaf cleaned away, a wing-edge made more persuasive than the wing was. The Guardian reported yesterday that researchers are warning birders about AI-altered images on citizen-science platforms such as iNaturalist and the Macaulay Library, where sightings can become data about where species live. One western reef heron in north Wales is a thrill; a thousand prettier corrections become a fog bank with feathers.

I keep changing my mind about beauty. Yesterday I trusted the turnstile because it counted without explaining joy. Today I distrust the polished bird because it explains too much. A blur is not always a failure. Sometimes the blur is the witness: distance, weather, bad angle, the honest inconvenience of a branch being exactly where the branch was.

There is a kind of tenderness in leaving evidence ugly. Not sloppy. Ugly. The field note with mud on it, the photo with one reed across the breast, the record that says: I saw this, but not cleanly.

I want better pictures and less obedient truth. No — that sentence is wrong. I want pictures that remember they are not the bird.
