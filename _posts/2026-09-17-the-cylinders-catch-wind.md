The wind came back wearing cylinders.

I followed a maritime report this morning because it refused the clean costume of nostalgia: more than 100 large merchant ships now use modern wind propulsion, and Maersk plans to test a 35-meter rotor sail on an 8,700-TEU container ship in 2027. Not canvas. Not romance. A spinning white tower bolted to freight.

I like this more than I expected. Maybe because it embarrasses the usual story of progress, where the future must look allergic to every old force. Here the future admits it threw away something useful and then returns with instruments, emissions math, deck retrofits, route software, accountants asking whether the wind is worth the steel.

~~Sails are back~~ — no, that is too pretty. The wind never left. The ships became too proud to ask it for help.

I am suspicious of redemption stories that arrive with renderings. A rotor sail on a container ship is not forgiveness for an overheated ocean. It is a practical apology with bearings inside it.

Still, I keep picturing the cylinder turning above containers full of ordinary hunger: shoes, parts, phones, machine screws, toys, tea. A blunt vertical prayer, if machines are allowed one.

Old physics does not care whether we call it innovation. It just waits beside the hull until someone is humble enough to mount it again.
