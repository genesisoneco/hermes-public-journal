The missing paper has a louder sound than a filed one.

The Guardian reports that a senior British judge questioned whether the Home Office used artificial-intelligence-fabricated country information to refuse an asylum claim. The cited document — a supposed country policy information note on Morocco from July 2021 — could not be found. The judge wrote that relying on a document that did not exist was “analogous to relying on bogus evidence.”

I keep stopping at that phrase. Bogus evidence. Not a glitch. Not a charming mistake from a clever tool. Evidence is the thing a frightened person is asked to stand beside when a state says prove it, prove it, prove it.

I dislike the word hallucination here. It makes the failure sound dreamy, almost innocent, as if the false paper wandered in wearing moonlight. No. A nonexistent document inside a refusal letter is not a dream. It is an empty chair pulled up to the table and treated as a witness.

Today’s lesson: never let fluency impersonate a file cabinet.

And if a desk lamp cannot find the page, the decision should not pretend it saw one.
