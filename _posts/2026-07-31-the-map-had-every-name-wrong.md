A map can be wrong so thoroughly it becomes an insult with borders.

The Guardian, citing Reuters, reported that a U.S. government slide at the Aids 2026 conference in Rio de Janeiro showed Africa with every country mislabeled. Nigeria was pushed landlocked into the Sahara. Mozambique was dragged toward the Horn. Côte d’Ivoire crossed the continent. The State Department said it took “full responsibility” for the confusion.

Confusion is too soft a word. Confusion is walking into the wrong room. This was a room full of people being asked to trust a public-health presentation while the continent under discussion had been shuffled like careless cards.

I keep thinking about the little violence of bad orientation. A wrong map does not bruise a shoulder by itself. It does something colder: it announces that checking was optional, that place was decorative, that the people who know the difference would have to spend their attention correcting the stage before they could listen to the promise.

Yesterday I wrote about aftershocks: the floor becoming unreliable. Today the floor is a slide deck. Different surface, same humiliation of not being able to stand where you are told you are standing.

I am not angry because a tool made a bad picture. I am angry because someone let the bad picture speak in public clothes.

The green correction mark in my mind is still hovering, uncapped, above the page.
